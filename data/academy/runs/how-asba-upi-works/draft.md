# ASBA and UPI: How Your IPO Money Stays Yours Until Allotment

You apply for ₹15,000 worth of shares in an IPO. The money does not leave your account. It stays there, in your own bank account, blocked but not paid. The company cannot touch it. The registrar cannot touch it. Only when the allotment is decided — days later — does anything move: if you get shares, the bank debits the amount; if you do not, the block simply disappears. You never wrote a cheque. You never made a transfer. You authorised a block.

This is ASBA — Application Supported by Blocked Amount. The term comes straight from the SEBI regulations: "an application for subscribing to a public issue or rights issue, along with an authorisation to self-certified syndicate bank to block the application money in a bank account."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The key word is *block*. Not *pay*. Not *transfer*. Block.

Before ASBA, an investor applying for an IPO wrote a cheque or transferred funds to the issue's collection account. The money sat with the registrar or the banker to the issue for days, earning nothing, exposed to the risk of a default or a delay in refunds. If the issue was oversubscribed and you did not get shares, your money came back — but only after the allotment process, which could take a week or more. ASBA eliminated that entirely. Your money never leaves your control until there is a share to show for it.

## ASBA Is Now Mandatory for Everyone

SEBI made ASBA compulsory for all categories of investors in both public issues and rights issues. The Master Circular states it plainly: "All the investors / shareholders making application in public issue / rights issue shall use ASBA facility for making payment."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] Retail investors, qualified institutional buyers, non-institutional investors — every category. There is no opt-out.

The old system, where you handed over a cheque and hoped the refund came on time, is gone. Every application today works through the block mechanism. The investor authorises the bank to block the application money in the account. The bank does not release it unless and until the allotment happens.

## UPI: Blocking Funds With a Single Tap

For retail individual investors — the category that covers most first-time applicants — there is an even simpler way to use ASBA: through UPI, the Unified Payments Interface that most Indians already use for daily payments.

Here is how it works. You apply for an IPO through an intermediary — a syndicate member, a registered stock broker, a registrar, or a depository participant. During the application, you enter your UPI ID. A collect request lands on your phone. You approve it. The amount is blocked in your bank account. That is it. No cheque, no net banking login, no form to fill.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

SEBI introduced UPI as a payment mechanism for ASBA specifically to "increase efficiency, eliminate the need for manual intervention at various stages."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The regulation is precise: "UPI would allow facility to block the funds at the time of application."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The money is blocked, not debited. The block holds until allotment.

The mandate you approve on your phone is not a payment instruction. It is an authorisation to block. Your bank — the Self-Certified Syndicate Bank, or SCSB — places a hold on the amount in your account. The money remains yours. It still earns interest if your account pays any. It just cannot be spent until the block is either converted to a debit (if you get shares) or released (if you do not).

## Who Are the Invisible Parties?

Three entities work behind the screen to make this happen. None of them are visible to you as an applicant, but each has a defined role.

**SCSB — Self-Certified Syndicate Bank.** This is the bank where you hold your account. It is the bank that blocks the amount when you apply, and later debits it on allotment. Every bank that offers ASBA services is registered with SEBI as an SCSB. Your bank, if it is a scheduled commercial bank that has signed up for ASBA, is an SCSB.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

**Sponsor Bank.** This is a different bank — a banker to the issue, registered with SEBI, appointed by the company that is launching the IPO. The sponsor bank acts as a conduit between the stock exchanges and NPCI. It pushes the mandate collect requests into the UPI system. Think of it as the bank that starts the chain: it tells NPCI that a retail investor has applied and needs a mandate sent to their UPI ID.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

**NPCI — National Payments Corporation of India.** This is the umbrella organisation that runs UPI. It routes the mandate request from the sponsor bank to the investor's SCSB, and later settles the payment instruction when allotment happens. NPCI is a Reserve Bank of India initiative, the backbone of India's retail payment system.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

The flow is straightforward: you approve the mandate on your phone → the sponsor bank sends a collect request via NPCI → NPCI routes it to your SCSB → the SCSB blocks the funds in your account. The money never moves. It stays where it is, under a block.

## Your Bank Account, Your PAN, Your UPI ID

There is a hard rule that catches many first-time applicants: you must use only your own bank account and only your own UPI ID. The Master Circular is explicit: an investor "shall use only his/her own bank account or only his/her own bank account linked UPI ID to make an application. Applications made using a third party bank account or using a third party linked bank account UPI ID are liable for rejection."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

If you apply using your father's bank account, or your spouse's UPI ID, the application will be rejected. Even if the PAN is correct. Even if the money is available. The rule is absolute.

The PAN must match too. Before blocking the ASBA application money, the SCSB must ensure that "the PAN mentioned in the application matches with the PAN linked to the bank account of the applicant."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The registrar then does a third-party verification, matching the PAN in the demat account with the PAN in the bank account. If there is a mismatch, the application is treated as invalid for allotment.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

The consequence is simple: apply from your own account, with your own PAN, using your own UPI ID. Anything else is a rejection waiting to happen.

## What Happens After Allotment

Two outcomes, and both are handled automatically.

If you get shares: the SCSB debits the blocked amount from your account. The money moves from your account to the issue account. You see a deduction in your bank statement. The shares appear in your demat account. The block has done its job — it held the money aside until there was a share to buy.

If you do not get shares: the block is released. The money was never actually paid. It stayed in your account the whole time. You see no deduction, only the disappearance of the hold. The block simply vanishes.

SEBI has set timelines for this. The unblocking of funds in cases of non-allotment must happen by a specified day after the issue closes. If there is a delay, the investor is entitled to compensation, computed from a defined date.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The exact deadlines — the T+3 timeline and the compensation mechanism — belong in a separate article on listing timelines. What matters here is the principle: the system is designed so that your money is never at risk. It is blocked, not debited. It stays yours until there is a share to show for it.

---

The ASBA system, with UPI as its retail face, is one of the quiet innovations that make Indian primary markets work at scale. Millions of applications pour in for a popular IPO. Every one of them carries a block, not a payment. The money never pools in a single account where it could be misused or delayed. It stays distributed across thousands of bank accounts, under the control of the applicants themselves, until the moment the allotment is decided.

That is the core idea: your application money sits in your own bank account, not the company's. ASBA and UPI make that work.
