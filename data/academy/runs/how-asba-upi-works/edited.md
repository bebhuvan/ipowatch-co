# ASBA and UPI: How Your IPO Money Stays Yours Until Allotment

ASBA lets an applicant block funds in their own bank account until IPO allotment is decided. The money does not leave the applicant's account. It stays there, blocked but not paid. The company cannot touch it. The registrar cannot touch it. Only when the allotment is decided, days later, does anything move: if shares are allotted, the bank debits the amount; if not, the block disappears. The applicant never wrote a cheque. No transfer was made. Only a block was authorised.

This is ASBA: Application Supported by Blocked Amount. The term comes straight from the SEBI regulations: "an application for subscribing to a public issue or rights issue, along with an authorisation to self-certified syndicate bank to block the application money in a bank account."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The key word is *block*. Not *pay*. Not *transfer*. Block.

Before ASBA, an investor applying for an IPO wrote a cheque or transferred funds to the issue's collection account. The money sat with the registrar or the banker to the issue for days, earning nothing, exposed to the risk of a default or a delay in refunds. If the issue was oversubscribed and the applicant did not get shares, the money came back but only after the allotment process, which could take a week or more. ASBA eliminated that. The applicant's money never leaves their control until there is a share to show for it.

## ASBA Is Now Mandatory for Everyone

SEBI made ASBA compulsory for all categories of investors in both public issues and rights issues. The Master Circular states it plainly: "All the investors / shareholders making application in public issue / rights issue shall use ASBA facility for making payment."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] Retail investors, qualified institutional buyers, non-institutional investors — every category. There is no opt-out.

The old system, where the investor handed over a cheque and hoped the refund came on time, is gone. Every application today works through the block mechanism. The investor authorises the bank to block the application money in the account. The bank does not release it unless allotment happens.

## UPI: Blocking Funds With a Single Tap

Retail individual investors — the category that covers most first-time applicants — have an even simpler way to use ASBA: through UPI, the Unified Payments Interface that most Indians already use for daily payments.

Here is how it works. The applicant applies for an IPO through an intermediary — a syndicate member, a registered stock broker, a registrar, or a depository participant. During the application, the applicant enters their UPI ID. A collect request lands on their phone. They approve it. The amount is blocked in their bank account. No cheque, no net banking login, no form to fill.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

SEBI introduced UPI as a payment mechanism for ASBA specifically to "increase efficiency, eliminate the need for manual intervention at various stages."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The regulation is precise: "UPI would allow facility to block the funds at the time of application."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The money is blocked, not debited. The block holds until allotment.

The mandate approved on the applicant's phone is not a payment instruction. It is an authorisation to block. The applicant's bank (the Self-Certified Syndicate Bank, or SCSB) places a hold on the amount in the applicant's account. The money remains in the account. It still earns interest if the account pays any. It just cannot be spent until the block is either converted to a debit (if shares are allotted) or released (if not).

## Who Are the Invisible Parties?

Three entities work behind the screen to make this happen. None of them are visible to the applicant, but each has a defined role.

**SCSB — Self-Certified Syndicate Bank.** This is the bank where the applicant holds their account. It is the bank that blocks the amount when the applicant applies, and later debits it on allotment. Every bank that offers ASBA services is registered with SEBI as an SCSB. The applicant's bank, if it is a scheduled commercial bank that has signed up for ASBA, is an SCSB.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

**Sponsor Bank.** This is a different bank — a banker to the issue, registered with SEBI, appointed by the company that is launching the IPO. The sponsor bank acts as a conduit between the stock exchanges and NPCI. It pushes the mandate collect requests into the UPI system. It acts as the bank that starts the chain: it tells NPCI that a retail investor has applied and needs a mandate sent to their UPI ID.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

**NPCI — National Payments Corporation of India.** This is the umbrella organisation that runs UPI. It routes the mandate request from the sponsor bank to the investor's SCSB, and later settles the payment instruction when allotment happens. NPCI is a Reserve Bank of India initiative, the backbone of India's retail payment system.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

The flow is straightforward: the applicant approves the mandate on their phone. The sponsor bank sends a collect request via NPCI. NPCI routes it to the applicant's SCSB. The SCSB blocks the funds in the applicant's account. The money never moves. It stays where it is, under a block.

## Your Bank Account, Your PAN, Your UPI ID

There is a hard rule that catches many first-time applicants: you must use only your own bank account and only your own UPI ID. The Master Circular is explicit: an investor "shall use only his/her own bank account or only his/her own bank account linked UPI ID to make an application. Applications made using a third party bank account or using a third party linked bank account UPI ID are liable for rejection."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

If you apply using your father's bank account, or your spouse's UPI ID, the application will be rejected. Even if the PAN is correct. Even if the money is available. The rule is absolute.

The PAN must match too. Before blocking the ASBA application money, the SCSB must ensure that "the PAN mentioned in the application matches with the PAN linked to the bank account of the applicant."[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] The registrar then does a third-party verification, matching the PAN in the demat account with the PAN in the bank account. If there is a mismatch, the application is treated as invalid for allotment.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline]

The consequence is simple: apply from your own account, with your own PAN, using your own UPI ID. Anything else is a rejection waiting to happen.

## What Happens After Allotment

Two outcomes, and both are handled automatically.

If shares are allotted: the SCSB debits the blocked amount from the applicant's account. The money moves from the applicant's account to the issue account. The deduction appears in the applicant's bank statement. The shares appear in their demat account. The block has done its job: it held the money aside until there was a share to buy.

If no shares are allotted: the block is released. The money was never actually paid. It stayed in the applicant's account the whole time. No deduction appears, only the disappearance of the hold. The block vanishes.

SEBI has set timelines for this. The unblocking of funds for non-allotment must happen by a specified day after the issue closes. If there is a delay, the investor is entitled to compensation, computed from a defined date.[^sebi-master-circular-asba-upi-timeline] What matters here is the principle: the system is designed so that the applicant's money is never at risk. It is blocked, not debited. It stays in the applicant's account until there is a share to show for it.
