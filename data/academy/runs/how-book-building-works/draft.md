# Book-building: how an IPO's price is set

A company going public does not walk in with a fixed price tag. It announces a range — a floor and a cap — and lets investors decide where in that range the shares should be priced. The process is called book-building, and it is how almost every Indian IPO works.

The alternative is a fixed-price issue, where the company names a single price upfront and investors either take it or leave it. Book-building is the opposite: the price is discovered from demand over a few days of bidding. The company and its merchant bankers collect bids from institutional investors, high-net-worth individuals, and retail applicants, then set the final price where the book is comfortably covered. That price is filed in the prospectus with the Registrar of Companies after the bidding closes.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding]

## The price band: what it is and the hard limits on it

The price band has two numbers. The floor is the lowest price at which the company will sell its shares. The cap is the highest. Investors bid anywhere in that range, and the final price lands somewhere between the two — or at the cap, if demand is strong enough.

SEBI puts hard limits on how wide that band can be. The cap cannot exceed 120% of the floor. If the floor is ₹100, the cap cannot go above ₹120. At the same time, the cap must be at least 105% of the floor — a band of ₹105 to ₹100 is the narrowest allowed.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] The rule prevents the company from setting a band so wide that it is meaningless, or so narrow that it defeats the purpose of price discovery.

There is also a floor below the floor. The floor price — and the final price — cannot be less than the face value of the share, the nominal value a company assigns each share when it is created. A company cannot price its IPO below that number.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding]

The company must announce the floor price or the price band at least two working days before the issue opens. The announcement is made through pre-issue and price band advertisements in the same newspapers where the public announcement of the issue was published.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] Those advertisements must include the relevant financial ratios — price-to-earnings, return on net worth, and so on — calculated for both ends of the band. They must also direct investors to the "basis of issue price" section of the offer document, where the company explains how it arrived at the range.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding]

| Constraint | Rule |
|---|---|
| Cap relative to floor | ≤ 120% of floor, ≥ 105% of floor |
| Floor relative to face value | Floor and final price ≥ face value |
| Advertisement timing | At least 2 working days before issue opens |
| Advertisement content | Financial ratios for both ends, reference to "basis of issue price" section |

## How long the IPO stays open

The bidding window has a minimum and a maximum. An IPO must be open for at least three working days and no more than ten working days.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] Within that range, the company chooses how long to keep the book open.

If the price band is revised during the issue — the company decides the range was too low or too high — the bidding period is extended by a minimum of three working days. The total window still cannot exceed ten working days.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] A revision is rare; it signals that the initial band missed the market's expectation badly enough that the company and its bankers decided to adjust.

Force majeure events — a banking strike, a natural disaster, a market crash that shuts the exchanges — can also extend the period. The issuer may add at least one working day, but must record the reasons in writing.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding]

## The minimum you can apply for

An investor cannot bid for a single share. The company sets a minimum application value — the smallest amount of money you must commit to enter the bidding. That minimum must fall between ₹10,000 and ₹15,000, and applications come in multiples of that lot.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] If the minimum is ₹12,000, you bid for ₹12,000, ₹24,000, ₹36,000, and so on.

The money you pay at the time of application depends on what kind of issue it is. For a fresh issue — where the company is issuing new shares and keeping the proceeds — you must pay at least 25% of the issue price per share at the time of application. For an offer for sale — where existing shareholders are selling their shares and the company gets nothing — you must pay the full issue price upfront.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding]

| Issue type | Payment at application |
|---|---|
| Fresh issue | At least 25% of issue price |
| Offer for sale | 100% of issue price |

## Who runs the book and underwrites it

The company does not run the book-building process itself. It appoints one or more merchant bankers as lead managers. Their names appear in the draft offer document and the final offer document.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] These are the institutions that design the price band, market the issue to institutional investors, collect bids, and recommend the final price.

The lead manager must also underwrite the issue. Underwriting is a guarantee: if the public does not buy enough shares, the lead manager buys the shortfall. The syndicate members — other banks and brokers who help sell the issue — sub-underwrite with the lead manager, but the lead manager is ultimately on the hook.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] This is not optional. The rule exists to ensure that someone with deep pockets is standing behind every IPO, so that a failed issue does not leave the company stranded without the capital it planned to raise.

## The floor on valid allotment

An IPO cannot proceed to allotment if fewer than 1,000 investors end up with shares. The rule is absolute: the issuer shall not make an allotment if the number of prospective allottees is less than one thousand.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] An issue that cannot attract a thousand allottees cannot be allotted at all.

For categories other than retail individual investors, non-institutional investors, and anchor investors — that is, for qualified institutional buyers (mutual funds, insurers, pension funds, foreign portfolio investors) and employees — allotment is done on a proportionate basis within the category: if QIBs bid for three times the shares reserved for them, each gets roughly one-third of what they applied for.[^sebi-icdr-2018-bookbuilding] Retail allotment in an oversubscribed issue works on a different principle, which the allotment piece covers in full.

The book-building process, from the price band to the final allotment, is designed to balance two things: letting the market set the price, and protecting the small investor from being squeezed out by the big money. The price band gives the company room to discover demand. The minimum application value keeps the door open for retail. The underwriting guarantee ensures the issue closes. And the 1,000-allottee floor prevents a company from listing with only a handful of shareholders — a situation that would make the stock illiquid and vulnerable to manipulation.
