# What Insider Trading Actually Means

The popular idea of insider trading is almost right, but the law is narrower and stranger. Most people think it means an employee buying or selling their own company's stock. That is not what the law bans. The prohibition targets something else entirely: the use or leak of information that the public does not have.

Insider trading, under Indian law, is either of two things. First, trading in a company's securities while in possession of important non-public information. Second, communicating that information to someone else except as part of a legitimate purpose. The person doing it does not have to be an employee. They do not have to work in finance. They just have to know something the market does not, and act on it or share it improperly.

> **Insider trading is not about who you are—it is about what you know.**

---

## What insider trading is not

Two misconceptions need clearing before the real rules make sense.

First, employees can own shares. There is no rule that says a person who works for a company cannot buy or hold its stock. The ban is on trading *while in possession of unpublished price-sensitive information*. An employee who knows nothing sensitive can buy shares freely. A director who knows the quarterly results are coming next week cannot.

Second, a person with a pre-arranged trading plan that complies with SEBI's rules can trade even if they are an insider. The plan is disclosed in advance, so the trade is not considered motivated by inside knowledge.[^sebi-pit-2015] This matters for company executives who are permanently in possession of sensitive information—they need a way to sell shares for legitimate reasons like tax planning or diversification without breaking the law.

---

## Who counts as an insider

The regulations define an insider in two ways, and the second is broader than most people expect.

The first category is a "connected person." This includes anyone who has been associated with the company in the prior six months in a way that gives access to unpublished price-sensitive information—directors, employees, advisers, contractual partners, even people who communicate frequently with officers. The definition is deliberately wide: it covers anyone who holds a position, including a professional or business relationship, that allows access to sensitive information or is reasonably expected to allow such access.[^sebi-pit-2015]

The second category is anyone who simply possesses or has access to unpublished price-sensitive information, whether they are connected or not.[^sebi-pit-2015] This is the catch-all. If a stranger accidentally overhears a confidential board meeting in a coffee shop and trades on what they heard, they are an insider under the regulations. The law does not care about your job title. It cares about what you know.

---

## What counts as unpublished price-sensitive information

The regulations call it UPSI—unpublished price-sensitive information. It has three elements.

First, it is information relating to a company or its securities, directly or indirectly. Second, it is not generally available—meaning it is not accessible to the public on a non-discriminatory basis.[^sebi-pit-2015] Third, if it became generally available, it would likely materially affect the share price.[^sebi-pit-2015] The information can be good or bad for the stock; the law treats both the same way.

The regulations give a non-exhaustive list of six categories that ordinarily qualify as UPSI:[^sebi-pit-2015]

| Category | Example |
|---|---|
| Financial results | Quarterly profit or loss figures before the exchange filing |
| Dividends | A surprise dividend announcement or a dividend cut |
| Change in capital structure | A bonus issue, stock split, or buyback |
| Mergers, acquisitions, major contracts | A takeover bid or a large order win outside normal business |
| Changes in key managerial personnel | The CFO resigning or being replaced |
| Changes in credit ratings | A rating upgrade or downgrade by a credit agency |

What does not count is common market news or publicly available analysis. The key test is whether the information has been released to the stock exchanges or published broadly. If it has, it is "generally available" and no longer UPSI.[^sebi-pit-2015] A newspaper report based on publicly filed documents is not inside information. A WhatsApp message with the same numbers before they are filed is.

---

## The two things an insider cannot do

The regulations create two independent prohibitions. Breaking either one is insider trading.

**Regulation 3 bans communicating UPSI.** No insider shall communicate, provide, or allow access to any unpublished price-sensitive information to any person, including other insiders, except where such communication is in furtherance of legitimate purposes, performance of duties, or discharge of legal obligations.[^sebi-pit-2015] Leaking a secret to a friend, casually mentioning upcoming results at a dinner party, or forwarding a confidential email to someone who does not need to know—all violate this rule. The prohibition applies even if the person who receives the information never trades on it. The leak itself is the offence.

**Regulation 4 bans trading while in possession of UPSI.** No insider shall trade in securities that are listed or proposed to be listed on a stock exchange when in possession of unpublished price-sensitive information.[^sebi-pit-2015] Trading includes buying, selling, pledging, or any other transaction. Simply holding a WhatsApp message with upcoming financial results and then selling shares can trigger this prohibition. The knowledge itself taints the trade.

The two prohibitions are independent. Even if you never trade, leaking UPSI is illegal. Conversely, even if you did not leak, trading while in possession of UPSI is illegal. You can violate one without violating the other.

---

## The presumption that makes the rule bite

The regulations create a legal presumption that shifts the burden of proof. When a person has traded in securities while in possession of UPSI, the trade is presumed to have been motivated by that knowledge and awareness.[^sebi-pit-2015] SEBI does not need to prove intent. The insider must prove innocence.

This makes enforcement practical. Proving what was going on inside someone's head when they placed a trade is nearly impossible. The presumption flips the problem: if you traded while you knew something the market did not, you are presumed guilty unless you can show otherwise.

Specific defences are allowed. For example, an off-market inter-se transfer between insiders who both possessed the same UPSI, or a transaction executed through the block-deal window, or a trade carried out under a pre-approved trading plan.[^sebi-pit-2015] The insider must demonstrate one of these circumstances or another valid reason. The burden is on them.

---

## How to trade while being an insider

An insider who may be permanently in possession of UPSI—a CFO who sees financial results months before they are published, a director who sits on the audit committee—faces a problem. They cannot trade at all under the general rule, because they are always in possession of some sensitive information. The regulations solve this with a safe harbour: the trading plan.

An insider can formulate a trading plan and present it to the company's compliance officer for approval and public disclosure. Once disclosed, the insider can trade automatically according to the plan without violating Regulation 4.[^sebi-pit-2015] The plan must be formulated in advance and cannot be changed once in effect. It covers trades over a specified period. Because the plan was created before the insider knew about upcoming UPSI, any trades are not considered motivated by inside knowledge.

This safe harbour is crucial for company insiders who need to sell shares for legitimate reasons like diversification or tax planning, without fear of enforcement. A CFO who wants to sell a portion of her holdings to pay for her children's education can set up a trading plan in January, disclose it, and execute the sale in June—even if the company's quarterly results in April were better than expected. The plan was set before she knew the results. The trade is compliant.

---

The law of insider trading in India is not about punishing employees for owning stock. It is about protecting the integrity of the market by ensuring that everyone trades on the same information at the same time. The person who knows the quarterly results before they are published has an unfair advantage. The regulations close that gap—not by banning knowledge, but by banning what you can do with it.

[^sebi-pit-2015]: SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015, last amended March 12, 2025.
