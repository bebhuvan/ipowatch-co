# PRIMARY MARKET MECHANICS

One company, many routes. A company that needs money — to build a factory, pay down debt, buy a competitor, or let its early investors cash out — has five ways to raise it from the equity market. The choice depends on three yes/no questions.

**Is the company already listed on a stock exchange?** An unlisted company can only do one thing: an IPO, its first public sale. A listed company has four options.

**Who is the company selling to?** Anyone with a brokerage account? Only the people who already own shares? Only the biggest institutions — mutual funds, insurers, pension funds?

**Are new shares being created, or are existing ones being transferred?** A fresh issue puts new money into the company's bank account. An offer for sale puts the money into the pockets of the selling shareholders — founders, early investors, private-equity funds — and the company gets nothing.

The main routes are the IPO, the FPO, the OFS, the rights issue, and the QIP. Each is a different answer to those three questions, and each is defined in the SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018 — the ICDR Regulations. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types]

---

## IPO – the first public issue

An IPO is the first time a company sells its shares to the public. Until then, the company is private: owned by its founders, a handful of early investors, maybe a venture-capital or private-equity fund. The IPO opens the door. Anyone with a brokerage account can buy in, and the company's shares start trading on the NSE and BSE.

Two things can happen in that sale, and usually both do. The company can issue new shares and keep the money — capital it uses to expand. And the early owners can sell some of their existing shares to the new public investors, cashing out part of a bet they made years earlier. The first is a *fresh issue*; the second is an *offer for sale*. An IPO can be either, or a combination of both.

The ICDR Regulations do not define "initial public offer" in their definitions chapter. The term is the heading of Chapter II, which governs the first public issue of specified securities by an issuer whose securities are not already listed. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types] Functionally, an IPO is the first public issue by an unlisted company. That is the only route an unlisted company can take.

---

## FPO – a public issue by a company already listed

An FPO — Further Public Offer — is a public issue of shares by a company that is already listed. Unlike an IPO, the company already has a market price, a trading history, and a set of public shareholders. It is coming back to the market for more.

An FPO can take three forms. The company can issue new shares and raise fresh capital. Existing shareholders can sell their shares to the public in an offer-for-sale component, with the proceeds going to them, not the company. Or it can be a combination of both.

The ICDR defines "further public offer" as an offer of specified securities by a listed issuer to the public for subscription, and it includes an offer for sale of specified securities to the public by any existing holders of such specified securities in a listed issuer. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types] The key point: it is a *public* offer — anyone can apply, just like an IPO — but the company is already listed.

---

## OFS – offer for sale: existing shares, no new capital

In an OFS, existing shareholders — not the company itself — sell their shares to the public. No new shares are created. The company receives nothing. The proceeds go entirely to the selling shareholders.

An OFS can appear in two contexts. It can be a component of an IPO or FPO, where the company issues fresh shares and the early investors sell some of theirs in the same transaction. Or it can be a standalone transaction on the exchange, often used by promoters to reduce their own stake — one way to meet the minimum public shareholding requirement that listed companies are subject to.

The ICDR defines "selling shareholder" as any shareholder of the issuer who is offering for sale the specified securities in a public issue. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types] An offer for sale is the sale of existing shares by such shareholders, in which no new shares are created and the company raises no money. The company is merely the platform; the sellers are the ones cashing out.

---

## Rights issue – offering shares to existing shareholders first

A rights issue gives existing shareholders the right — but not the obligation — to buy new shares in proportion to their current holding. It is not open to the general public. Only the people who already own the company's shares on a specific date get the offer.

The company sets a record date. Every shareholder on that date receives a rights entitlement: for every share they hold, they can buy a certain number of new shares at a fixed price, usually at a discount to the market price. They have three choices. They can subscribe and buy the shares. They can sell the rights in the open market to someone who wants them. Or they can let the rights lapse, in which case the unsubscribed portion may be taken up by other shareholders or by the underwriters.

The ICDR defines "rights issue" as an offer of specified securities by a listed issuer to the shareholders of the issuer as on the record date fixed for the said purpose. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types] The defining feature is exclusivity: the offer goes only to existing shareholders, not to the public.

## QIP – a fast private placement to big institutions

A Qualified Institutions Placement (QIP) is a private placement: a listed company issues shares only to Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) — the big money. Mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds, foreign portfolio investors, banks, and other entities that SEBI considers sophisticated enough to fend for themselves.

A QIP is the quick route. Because it is a private placement to institutions, it bypasses the full public-offer machinery — the draft red herring prospectus, the public bidding window, the retail allotment process. The trade-off is who can buy: a QIP is open only to qualified institutional buyers, not to retail investors or high-net-worth individuals. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types]

The ICDR defines "qualified institutions placement" as the issue of eligible securities by a listed issuer to qualified institutional buyers on a private placement basis, and it includes an offer for sale of specified securities by the promoters or promoter group on a private placement basis. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types]

A QIP requires shareholder approval. Regulation 172(1) says a listed issuer may make a QIP only if a special resolution approving the placement has been passed by its shareholders, and the special resolution must specify that the allotment is proposed to be made through a QIP. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types] There is one exception: no shareholders' resolution is required if the QIP is an offer for sale by promoters or promoter group for compliance with minimum public shareholding requirements. [^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types] In that case, the promoters are selling their own shares to QIBs to reduce their stake, and the company does not need a vote.

## How they compare: the three-question map

The three questions that sort every route:

**Is the company listed?** An unlisted company can only do an IPO. A listed company can do an FPO, an OFS, a rights issue, or a QIP.

**Who is it selling to?** IPO and FPO are public issues — anyone can apply. A rights issue is limited to existing shareholders as on a record date. A QIP is limited to Qualified Institutional Buyers. An OFS, when standalone, is typically open to all investors on the exchange.

**Are new shares created?** IPO (fresh portion), FPO (fresh portion), and rights issue create new shares — the company gets new capital. OFS and QIP (when structured as an OFS by promoters) transfer existing shares — the company gets nothing.

| Route | Company status | Buyers | New shares? |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPO | Unlisted | Public | Yes (fresh portion) or no (OFS portion) |
| FPO | Listed | Public | Yes (fresh portion) or no (OFS portion) |
| OFS | Listed | Public (exchange) | No |
| Rights issue | Listed | Existing shareholders only | Yes |
| QIP | Listed | QIBs only | Yes (fresh) or no (OFS variant) |

One route is deliberately left out of this map: the buyback, where a company buys back its own shares from shareholders, returning capital instead of raising it. That is the reverse operation — a capital-return route, not a capital-raising one — and it has its own set of rules under the SEBI Buyback Regulations. It is covered in a separate article.

The five routes here cover every way a company raises equity capital from the market. The three questions — listed or not, who buys, new shares or old — are the map. Everything else is detail.

[^sebi-icdr-2018-issue-types]: SEBI ICDR Regulations, 2018 — definitions of the public-issue family (IPO, FPO, OFS, QIP, rights, preferential) and QIP eligibility (Reg 172). Securities and Exchange Board of India, last amended March 8, 2025.
