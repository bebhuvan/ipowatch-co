# What is a buyback: The mirror image of an IPO

A buyback is a company using its own cash to buy its own shares from its shareholders, then cancelling them. It is the mirror image of an IPO: instead of raising capital by selling new shares to the public, the company returns capital by buying existing shares back. The share count shrinks. Each remaining shareholder owns a larger slice of the same company.

Take XYZ Ltd. Five years ago it raised ₹100 crore in its IPO. Now it has extra cash — more than it needs for its operations and planned investments. It decides to buy back 10% of its shares for ₹20 crore. After the buyback, those shares are cancelled. The shareholders who sold get cash. The ones who held on now own a bigger percentage of XYZ Ltd without having spent a rupee. The company is smaller in terms of share capital, but the remaining shareholders have a larger claim on its profits and assets.

This is not a casual market transaction. SEBI's Buy-Back of Securities Regulations, 2018 govern the entire process. The regulations define a "tender offer" as an offer by a company to buy back its own shares through a letter of offer from existing holders.[^sebi-buyback-2018] The buyback is a regulated corporate action, not a simple purchase on the stock exchange.

---

## Two ways to buy back

SEBI allows a company to buy back its shares by one of two methods, and it must pick only one per buyback.[^sebi-buyback-2018]

**The tender offer.** The company issues a letter of offer to all shareholders, inviting them to sell a specified number of shares at a fixed price. Each shareholder can tender up to their proportionate share — if the company wants to buy back 10% of its shares, each shareholder can sell up to 10% of what they hold. If too many shareholders offer their shares (the buyback is oversubscribed), the company buys on a proportionate basis, so everyone who tendered gets to sell the same fraction of what they offered. This method is fair by design: every shareholder gets equal treatment.

**The open-market route.** The company buys its own shares on the stock exchange, just like any other investor. But it cannot buy without limit. SEBI has set volume caps on how much a company can buy through the stock-exchange route, and these caps have been phased down over time — from higher percentages of paid-up capital and free reserves to lower ones, in stages.[^sebi-buyback-2018] The principle is clear: the regulator wants to prevent the company from distorting the market price by buying too aggressively.

The company cannot mix the two methods. The board or shareholders must approve which method to use before the buyback begins.

| Aspect | Tender offer | Open market |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Company invites shareholders to sell at a fixed price, proportionately | Company buys shares on the stock exchange like any investor |
| Pricing | Fixed price set in advance | Market price at time of purchase |
| Fairness | Equal treatment — all shareholders can participate proportionately | Only shareholders who sell on the exchange participate |
| Volume limit | Up to 25% of paid-up capital and free reserves | Subject to phased-down volume caps set by SEBI |

## The 25% cap and the debt guardrail

A buyback cannot be unlimited. SEBI imposes two financial limits that work together to keep the company from overreaching.

**The 25% cap.** A company cannot buy back more than 25% of the aggregate of its paid-up capital and free reserves. This is the maximum, and it is calculated on the lower of the company's standalone or consolidated financial statements.[^sebi-buyback-2018] If the standalone numbers give a lower figure, that is the one that counts. The cap prevents the company from using too much of its equity base for a buyback.

**The debt-to-capital ratio.** After the buyback, the company's total debt — secured and unsecured combined — must be no more than twice its paid-up capital and free reserves. This is the 2:1 guardrail.[^sebi-buyback-2018] It ensures the company does not load up on debt to fund the buyback. If a company already has high debt, it may not be able to do a buyback at all, because the post-buyback ratio would exceed 2:1. The ratio is also calculated on the lower of standalone or consolidated financials.

There is one exception: if the Companies Act, 2013 has notified a higher debt-to-capital ratio for a particular class of companies, that higher ratio prevails.[^sebi-buyback-2018] But for most companies, 2:1 is the ceiling.

Only fully paid-up shares can be bought back. A company cannot buy back partly paid shares, because those are not fully owned by the shareholder.[^sebi-buyback-2018]

## Where the money comes from

A company cannot use just any cash it has lying around. The regulation specifies three allowed sources, and the company must pick one or more of them:[^sebi-buyback-2018]

- **Free reserves** — retained earnings that the company has accumulated over time but not distributed as dividends or used for other purposes.
- **The securities premium account** — money collected above the face value of shares from previous issues. If a company issued shares at ₹100 when the face value was ₹10, the ₹90 premium went into this account.
- **The proceeds of a fresh issue of shares** — money raised by issuing new shares specifically for the purpose of the buyback.

There is a restriction: the buyback cannot be funded out of the proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind of shares.[^sebi-buyback-2018] If the company raised money through a rights issue of equity shares, it cannot use that same pool of money to buy back equity shares. This prevents the company from effectively returning the capital it just raised, which would defeat the purpose of the earlier issue.

## Cancelling the shares and anti-avoidance rules

The company does not get to keep the shares it buys back. They must be extinguished and physically destroyed within seven working days after the buyback period ends, and the buyback is not allowed unless the consequent reduction of share capital is actually effected.[^sebi-buyback-2018] The share capital is genuinely reduced, not just parked. The company's balance sheet shrinks, and the remaining shares represent a larger claim on the company's assets and earnings.

SEBI also prohibits indirect buybacks. A company cannot buy its own shares through a subsidiary company or through any investment company or group of investment companies.[^sebi-buyback-2018] This anti-avoidance rule prevents the company from circumventing the limits and disclosure requirements by using a related entity to do the buying.

The entire buyback must be completed within one year from the date of board or shareholder approval. After that, the company cannot extend it without fresh approval.[^sebi-buyback-2018]

[^sebi-buyback-2018]: SEBI (Buy-Back of Securities) Regulations, 2018, last amended November 28, 2024.
