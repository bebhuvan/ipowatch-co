# What Is an IPO — and Who Gets to Do One in India

An IPO is the first time a company sells its shares to the public. Before that, it's private: owned by founders, a clutch of early investors, maybe a VC or PE fund. The IPO flings the door open. Anyone with a brokerage account can buy in, and a week later the shares trade on the NSE or BSE. The company is now *listed*.

Two things happen in that sale, and usually both. The company can issue brand-new shares and keep the money — to pay down debt, build a factory, fund R&D, buy a rival. That's a *fresh issue*. Separately, existing shareholders — founders, angels, PE backers — can sell some of their existing shares to the new public investors, cashing out a bet they made years ago. That's an *offer for sale*. Most Indian IPOs are a mix.

None of this is casual. A company can't just decide to list. SEBI — the Securities and Exchange Board of India, the market regulator — lays out exactly who is kept out and who qualifies. The rest of this piece is about that gate: why a company goes through it, what changes on the other side, who is barred from trying, and the two routes SEBI provides for those allowed through.

---

## Why a Company Goes Public

The obvious reason is money. The fresh-issue portion of an IPO brings capital the company can put to work immediately — paying off loans, building factories, funding research, buying other businesses. A company that grew on borrowed money can use the proceeds to clean up its balance sheet.

The second reason is a payout for early backers. Founders who started the company a decade ago, angels who wrote the first cheque, PE funds that held the stock for years — all get a chance to sell part of their stake through the offer-for-sale portion. The IPO turns paper wealth into cash.

Other advantages: A public company can use its shares as currency for acquisitions — instead of paying cash, it can offer stock. It can attract better talent by granting stock options employees can actually sell one day. And banks and bond markets tend to offer cheaper financing to listed companies, because ongoing disclosure gives lenders a clearer picture of the company's health.

## Life After the IPO

Once the shares trade on the exchange, the company enters a different world.

The shares trade daily. Anyone with a brokerage account — a retail investor in Chennai, a mutual fund in Mumbai, a foreign pension fund in New York — can buy or sell, and the price moves on supply, demand, and news. Ownership becomes dispersed among thousands of public shareholders. The company now has a *public float* — shares held by the public, not promoters — and must answer to minority investors who have no relationship with the founders.

The biggest change is disclosure. A private company files its accounts once a year with the ROC, and that's largely it. A listed company must file quarterly results, annual reports, details of every related-party transaction, and immediate disclosures of any material event — a big order win, a factory fire, a promoter selling shares — to the stock exchanges. The compliance burden is substantial. And it never ends.

## Who Cannot Do an IPO

SEBI keeps certain entities out of the IPO market entirely. The list lives in Regulation 5 of the ICDR Regulations — the Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements rules that govern all public offers.

First, anyone currently debarred from the capital market by SEBI is out. The company itself, its promoters, its directors, any selling shareholders under a SEBI ban. The restriction also extends to a promoter or director linked to another company that is debarred. A promoter of Company A cannot take it public if they are also associated with Company B, which is banned.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

Second, wilful defaulters and fraudulent borrowers — as defined by the RBI — are barred. So are fugitive economic offenders, declared under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

Third, an IPO cannot happen if the company has any outstanding convertible securities — instruments like convertible bonds or warrants that could later become equity shares. The logic: if someone holds a right to convert after the IPO, the public investors don't know exactly how many shares will exist. Two exceptions: employee stock options and stock appreciation rights are allowed, and any other convertible securities must be fully converted before the prospectus is filed.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

A past debarment is not permanent. If the period has ended by the time the draft offer document is filed with SEBI, the restriction no longer applies. The ban is not a permanent stain; it expires with the penalty.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

| Category | What it means |
|---|---|
| Debarred by SEBI | Company, promoters, directors, or selling shareholders currently banned from accessing capital markets |
| Wilful defaulter / fraudulent borrower | Defined by RBI; includes those who have defaulted despite having the ability to pay |
| Fugitive economic offender | Declared under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 |
| Outstanding convertible securities | Bonds, warrants, or other instruments that could convert into equity — must be converted or cancelled before IPO |

## The Two Ways to Qualify

A company that is not barred still has to prove it is fit to list. SEBI provides two routes. They are mutually exclusive: a company either qualifies under the first or must use the second.

### Route One: The Profitability Route

This is the straightforward path. The company must show it already makes money and has done so consistently.

The thresholds, all calculated on a restated and consolidated basis for the preceding three full years:

- **Net tangible assets** of at least ₹3 crore in each of the three years, with no more than half of those assets held in cash or cash equivalents — unless the excess cash is already committed to the business or project.
- **Average operating profit** of at least ₹15 crore over the three years, with a profit in each of those three years — not just an average that hides a loss year.
- **Net worth** of at least ₹1 crore in each of the three years.

One more rule: if the company changed its name in the last year, at least half of its revenue must come from the activity reflected in the new name. This prevents a company from rebranding into a hot sector just before an IPO.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

A company meeting these thresholds can use any issue method — fixed price or book-building — and the allocation rules are relatively flexible: at least 35% of the net offer to retail investors, at least 15% to non-institutional investors, and up to 50% to qualified institutional buyers.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

### Route Two: The Compulsory-QIB Route

If the company does not meet the profitability thresholds, it can still do an IPO — but only through the book-building process, and with a much stricter allocation rule. It must allot at least 75% of the net offer to qualified institutional buyers — the big money: mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds, foreign portfolio investors. If it cannot achieve that allotment, it must refund the entire subscription amount. No partial success.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

The allocation for retail investors under this route is capped at 10% of the net offer, and for non-institutional investors at 15%.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

The logic is protective. A company that has not proven it can generate consistent profits is a riskier bet. By forcing it to sell mostly to sophisticated institutions — who have the resources to do their own due diligence — SEBI ensures the risk is underwritten by those best equipped to judge it. Retail investors, who may not have the same analytical capability, are limited to a small portion.

| Requirement | Route 1 (Profitability) | Route 2 (Compulsory QIB) |
|---|---|---|
| Net tangible assets | At least ₹3 crore in each of 3 preceding years | Not required |
| Operating profit | At least ₹15 crore average, profit each year | Not required |
| Net worth | At least ₹1 crore in each of 3 preceding years | Not required |
| Issue method | Any | Book-building only |
| Minimum QIB allocation | Up to 50% of net offer | At least 75% of net offer |
| Maximum retail allocation | At least 35% of net offer | Up to 10% of net offer |

## IPO vs. FPO vs. OFS: What's the Difference?

These three terms get confused often, but the distinction is straightforward once you ask two questions: *Has this stock ever traded publicly before?* and *Are the shares coming from the company or from its shareholders?*

**IPO** — initial public offer — is the first public sale by a company that is not yet listed. That's the subject of this entire article.

**FPO** — follow-on public offer — is a public sale by a company that is already listed. It can be a fresh issue of new shares, an offer for sale by existing shareholders, or both. The ICDR Regulations define it as "an offer of specified securities by a listed issuer to the public for subscription" and explicitly include an offer for sale by existing holders in a listed issuer.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

**OFS** — offer for sale — is a specific type of public sale where existing shareholders, not the company, sell their shares. No new shares are created, so the company does not raise any money; the selling shareholders do. An OFS can happen as part of an IPO (the offer-for-sale portion of the overall offering) or as part of an FPO. The ICDR Regulations define a "selling shareholder" as any shareholder of the issuer who is offering shares for sale in a public issue.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

In short: IPO is the first listing; FPO is a subsequent public sale by a listed company; OFS is a sale of existing shares, not new ones. The company raises money only in a fresh issue, whether that happens in an IPO or an FPO. The selling shareholders raise money in an OFS, wherever it occurs.

---

The gate to the public market is not wide open. SEBI keeps out those it has banned, those who have defaulted on their debts, those who have fled the country with stolen money, and those whose capital structure is still in flux with convertible instruments. For those who are allowed, the path splits: prove you already make money, or sell mostly to institutions who can judge the risk themselves. Every company that lists in India walks through one of those two doors.
