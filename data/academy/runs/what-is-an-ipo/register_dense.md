# What Is an IPO — and Who Gets to Do One in India

An initial public offer is the first sale of a company's equity shares to the public. Before that sale, the company is private: owned by founders, early investors, and possibly a venture-capital or private-equity fund. After the sale, the shares trade on the National Stock Exchange or the BSE. The company becomes *listed*.

Two distinct transactions occur in that sale, and most Indian IPOs combine both. The company may issue new shares and retain the proceeds—capital for debt repayment, factory construction, research, or acquisitions. That is a *fresh issue*. Separately, existing shareholders—founders, angel investors, private-equity backers—may sell some of their existing shares to new public investors, monetising a prior investment. That is an *offer for sale*.

None of this is casual. A company cannot simply decide to list. The Securities and Exchange Board of India—SEBI, the market regulator—prescribes who is excluded and who qualifies. This article examines that gate: why a company enters it, what changes on the other side, who is barred from attempting it, and the two routes SEBI provides for those permitted.

---

## Why a Company Goes Public

The primary reason is capital. The fresh-issue portion of an IPO provides funds the company can deploy immediately—repaying loans, building factories, funding research, or acquiring other businesses. A company that has grown on borrowed money can use IPO proceeds to clean its balance sheet.

The second reason is liquidity for early backers. Founders who started the company a decade ago, angel investors who wrote the first cheque, and private-equity funds that have held the stock for years all gain an exit through the offer-for-sale portion. The IPO converts paper wealth into cash.

Other advantages exist. A public company can use its shares as acquisition currency—offering stock instead of cash. It can attract talent by granting stock options that employees can eventually sell. Banks and bond markets tend to offer cheaper financing to listed companies, because ongoing disclosure requirements give lenders a clearer picture of the company's health.

---

## Life After the IPO

Once shares begin trading on the exchange, the company enters a different regulatory and operational environment.

The shares trade daily. Anyone with a brokerage account—a retail investor in Chennai, a mutual fund in Mumbai, a foreign pension fund in New York—can buy or sell, and the price moves on supply, demand, and news. Ownership becomes dispersed among thousands of public shareholders. The company acquires a *public float*—shares held by the public, not the promoters—and must answer to minority investors who have no relationship with the founders.

The most significant change is disclosure. A private company files accounts once a year with the Registrar of Companies; that is largely it. A listed company must file quarterly results, annual reports, details of every related-party transaction, and immediate disclosures of any material event—a major order win, a factory fire, a promoter selling shares—to the stock exchanges. The compliance burden is substantial and perpetual.

## Who Cannot Do an IPO

SEBI excludes certain entities from the IPO market entirely. The list appears in Regulation 5 of the ICDR Regulations—the Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements rules governing all public offers.

First, anyone currently debarred from the capital market by SEBI is ineligible. That includes the company, its promoters, its directors, and any selling shareholders under a SEBI ban. The restriction extends to a promoter or director associated with another debarred company. A promoter of Company A cannot take it public if they are also connected to Company B, which is banned.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

Second, wilful defaulters and fraudulent borrowers—as defined by the Reserve Bank of India—are barred. So are fugitive economic offenders, declared under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

Third, an IPO cannot proceed if the company has any outstanding convertible securities—instruments such as convertible bonds or warrants that could later be converted into equity shares. The logic: if someone holds a conversion right after the IPO, public investors cannot know the final share count. Two exceptions exist: employee stock options and stock appreciation rights are permitted; all other convertible securities must be fully converted before the prospectus is filed.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

A past debarment is not permanent. If the period has expired by the time the draft offer document is filed with SEBI, the restriction no longer applies. The ban expires with the penalty; it is not a permanent stain.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

| Category | What it means |
|---|---|
| Debarred by SEBI | Company, promoters, directors, or selling shareholders currently banned from accessing capital markets |
| Wilful defaulter / fraudulent borrower | Defined by RBI; includes those who have defaulted despite having the ability to pay |
| Fugitive economic offender | Declared under the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act, 2018 |
| Outstanding convertible securities | Bonds, warrants, or other instruments that could convert into equity — must be converted or cancelled before IPO |

## The Two Ways to Qualify

A company not barred must still demonstrate fitness to list. SEBI provides two routes; they are mutually exclusive. A company either qualifies under the first or must use the second.

### Route One: The Profitability Route

This is the straightforward path. The company must show it already generates profits and has done so consistently.

The thresholds, all calculated on a restated and consolidated basis for the preceding three full years:

- **Net tangible assets** of at least ₹3 crore in each of the three years, with no more than half of those assets held in cash or cash equivalents—unless the excess cash is already committed to the business or project.
- **Average operating profit** of at least ₹15 crore over the three years, with a profit in each of those three years—not merely an average that conceals a loss year.
- **Net worth** of at least ₹1 crore in each of the three years.

One additional rule: if the company changed its name in the last year, at least half of its revenue must come from the activity reflected in the new name. This prevents a company from rebranding into a hot sector just before an IPO.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

A company meeting these thresholds may use any issue method—fixed price or book-building—and the allocation rules are relatively flexible: at least 35% of the net offer to retail investors, at least 15% to non-institutional investors, and up to 50% to qualified institutional buyers.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

### Route Two: The Compulsory-QIB Route

If the company does not meet the profitability thresholds, it may still conduct an IPO—but only through the book-building process, and with a stricter allocation rule. It must allot at least 75% of the net offer to qualified institutional buyers—mutual funds, insurance companies, pension funds, foreign portfolio investors. If it cannot achieve that allotment, it must refund the entire subscription amount. No partial success.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

The allocation for retail investors under this route is capped at 10% of the net offer; for non-institutional investors, at 15%.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

The logic is protective. A company that has not proven consistent profit generation is a riskier bet. By forcing it to sell mostly to sophisticated institutions—which have the resources to conduct their own due diligence—SEBI ensures the risk is underwritten by those best equipped to judge it. Retail investors, who may lack the same analytical capability, are limited to a small portion.

| Requirement | Route 1 (Profitability) | Route 2 (Compulsory QIB) |
|---|---|---|
| Net tangible assets | At least ₹3 crore in each of 3 preceding years | Not required |
| Operating profit | At least ₹15 crore average, profit each year | Not required |
| Net worth | At least ₹1 crore in each of 3 preceding years | Not required |
| Issue method | Any | Book-building only |
| Minimum QIB allocation | Up to 50% of net offer | At least 75% of net offer |
| Maximum retail allocation | At least 35% of net offer | Up to 10% of net offer |

## IPO vs. FPO vs. OFS: What's the Difference?

These three terms are often confused. The distinction becomes clear once two questions are asked: *Has this stock ever traded publicly before?* and *Are the shares coming from the company or from its shareholders?*

**IPO**—initial public offer—is the first public sale by a company not yet listed. It is the subject of this entire article.

**FPO**—follow-on public offer—is a public sale by a company that is already listed. It can be a fresh issue of new shares, an offer for sale by existing shareholders, or both. The ICDR Regulations define it as "an offer of specified securities by a listed issuer to the public for subscription" and explicitly include an offer for sale by existing holders in a listed issuer.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

**OFS**—offer for sale—is a specific type of public sale where existing shareholders, not the company, sell their shares. No new shares are created; the company does not raise money; the selling shareholders do. An OFS can occur as part of an IPO (the offer-for-sale portion of the overall offering) or as part of an FPO. The ICDR Regulations define a "selling shareholder" as any shareholder of the issuer who offers shares for sale in a public issue.[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]

In short: IPO is the first listing; FPO is a subsequent public sale by a listed company; OFS is a sale of existing shares, not new ones. The company raises money only in a fresh issue, whether in an IPO or an FPO. The selling shareholders raise money in an OFS, wherever it occurs.

---

The gate to the public market is not wide open. SEBI excludes those it has banned, those who have defaulted on debts, those declared fugitive economic offenders, and those whose capital structure remains in flux with convertible instruments. For those permitted, the path splits: prove you already make money, or sell mostly to institutions capable of judging the risk themselves. Every company that lists in India walks through one of those two doors.

[^sebi-icdr-2018-2025-03-08]: Securities and Exchange Board of India (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018, last amended March 8, 2025.
