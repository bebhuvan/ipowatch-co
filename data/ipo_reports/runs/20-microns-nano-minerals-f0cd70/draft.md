## What the company does

The core of the business is processing, not extraction. The company takes minerals and turns them into specialty chemicals — products engineered for specific industrial functions rather than sold as raw commodities. Its product range splits into four buckets: Functional Additives and Chemically Modified Minerals, which the filing calls the principal specialty-chemical business, plus Soft Minerals and Hard Minerals. [^drhp-p112] In plain terms, these are materials that get added to other products during manufacturing — a paint maker, a plastics processor, or a cosmetics formulator might buy them to change how their own product behaves.

The most important structural fact about this company is how it sells. It does not run its own sales force. A long-term arrangement gives 20 Microns Limited, the corporate promoter, the job of marketing and selling the company's products over its network. The filing calls this a source of substantial synergies. [^drhp-p112] [^drhp-p113] That arrangement is a double-edged sword: it gives the company access to an established distribution network without building one, but it also makes the company's revenue dependent on a related party's sales effort.

Customers do not sign long-term contracts. Sales are governed by purchase orders, so the volume, pricing, and terms of every order are set individually. The filing lists this as a risk factor. [^drhp-p26] That makes customer concentration important: the top ten customers produced about 59% of revenue from operations in FY2015, 57% in FY2016, and 59% in FY2017. [^drhp-p128] A business with no contracts and nearly three-fifths of revenue coming from ten buyers is exposed to the loss of any single customer.

Exports are a small second channel. The filing says exports of ₹1,068.18 lakh (₹10.68 crore) were achieved over the past five years, to Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, and Sri Lanka. [^drhp-p128] That is a modest figure against the company's asset base, and it is concentrated in three countries.

The physical footprint is two manufacturing locations — GIDC Waghodia in Gujarat and Pathur Village, Tirunelveli in Tamil Nadu — plus administrative offices in Bhuj and Udaipur. The company does not own the premises of those plants and offices. [^drhp-p20] That is a governance flag worth noting: the company's main productive assets sit on land it does not own.

The filing's own list of strengths: a diversified product portfolio, diversified user industries, import substitution, support of the holding company, continuous R&D, and a strong management team. ## The market: specialty chemicals in India

The company sits in India's specialty chemicals industry. Specialty chemicals are the value-added end of the chemical business — products made for specific functions rather than bulk commodities. The DRHP says this segment holds roughly 17% of the Indian chemicals industry, about ₹1,000 billion, and separately cites a ₹1,443 billion market figure. Growth is double-digit. The specialty chemicals segment grew at 10-12% CAGR from fiscal 2014 to 2017, and CRISIL Research forecasts 12-14% CAGR going forward. [^drhp-p94] Within that, the filing's fastest niches are construction chemicals, water chemicals, and personal-care ingredients, each expected to grow at 15% CAGR; surfactants at 13%; textile chemicals and flavours & fragrances at 12%; polymer additives at 10%; polymer demand at 9-10% and plastic products at 11-13%. [^drhp-p94] [^drhp-p103] The company's own product lines — functional additives and modified minerals — sit closest to the polymer and plastics end of that spectrum.

Competition is fragmented. SMEs hold 70-75% of the market, and only a few Indian players have the scale to take on global giants on product development and innovation. [^drhp-p94] [^drhp-p95] That fragmentation cuts both ways: it means room for small players, but it also means pricing pressure from a crowded field. The filing acknowledges this directly, listing growing competition and consequent pricing pressure as a risk. [^drhp-p27]

Demand drivers are macro. India's GDP grew 7.1% in FY2017, private consumption accounts for 58% of GDP, urbanisation is rising, the DRHP sees the middle class reaching 148 million households by 2030, and the government has drafted a National Chemical Policy while allowing 100% FDI in the sector. [^drhp-p87] [^drhp-p88] [^drhp-p90] [^drhp-p95] [^drhp-p96] These are the tailwinds the filing points to: a growing economy, a rising consuming class, and a policy environment friendly to chemical manufacturing.

Import substitution is the company's stated hook. Chemicals imports grew at 6.4% CAGR against exports at 6% CAGR, and China's stricter environmental rules since 2015 have reduced its chemical manufacturing output. [^drhp-p92] [^drhp-p96] The logic: if China makes less and India imports more, an Indian processor that can replace imports has a runway. The filing also notes that industrial licensing has been done away with for most chemicals except a small list of hazardous ones. [^drhp-p95]

The headwinds the filing acknowledges: imports consistently exceeding exports, a fragmented market that invites pricing pressure, strict EU regulation under REACH for exporters, and changing tax and regulatory laws. [^drhp-p92] [^drhp-p94] [^drhp-p110] [^drhp-p33] REACH — Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals — is the EU's regulatory regime for chemical imports, and it matters for any Indian exporter selling into Europe.

## The material risks

**Customer concentration without contracts.** The top ten customers bring roughly 59% of revenue, and sales are on purchase orders with no long-term agreements. [^drhp-p26] [^drhp-p128] One lost customer or one renegotiated price hits revenue immediately. There is no contract pipeline to smooth the blow.

**Negative cash flow in a profitable year.** FY2017 net profit was ₹1.75 crore but operating cash flow was -₹3.50 crore. [^drhp-p49] [^drhp-p50] The DRHP itself calls out negative cash flows in previous years as a risk factor. [^drhp-p22] A company that reports profit but burns cash is either growing fast or managing working capital poorly; the filing does not say which.

**Regulatory gaps.** Certain statutory licences, registrations, and approvals needed to operate the factory at Plot 9-10, GIDC Waghodia are not yet obtained, and there have been past RoC filing lapses — so penalties remain possible. [^drhp-p19] [^drhp-p18] The company cannot assure it will not be penalised for non-compliance under the relevant laws.

[^drhp-p112] [^drhp-p113] Any disruption to that network or relationship would expose the company's revenue directly. The company has no independent sales infrastructure of its own.
