A B Infrabuild Limited is an infrastructure contractor and ready-mix concrete producer [^drhp-p63]. It builds railway stations, bridges and roads for government bodies, and it sells concrete from a plant at Kashimira in Thane, Maharashtra [^drhp-p63-71]. The company is offering 44,28,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at a price that has not yet been fixed, with proceeds earmarked mainly for working capital [^drhp-p29-48]. The rest of this report covers the business, the market, the five-year financial history disclosed in the DRHP, and the risks the filing itself flags.

## What the company does

A B Infrabuild makes money in two ways. It creates infrastructure by executing contracts for railways, bridges and roads, and it operates a ready-mix concrete (RMC) plant [^drhp-p63]. On the infrastructure side, the work spans railway contracts such as designing and constructing platforms, station buildings, gauge conversion and laying railway tracks; bridge works across beam, cantilever, arch, suspension, cable-stayed and truss forms; and construction of bituminous and concrete roads [^drhp-p71]. The filing describes the RMC plant at Kashimira in Thane District, Maharashtra, as a modern facility [^drhp-p67].

The business runs on tenders. A client floats a tender for a specific contract, a pre-bid meeting is held to resolve queries, the contractor bids, and the job is awarded to the lowest quote. The winning contractor then deposits a performance guarantee, after which drawings are issued and the site is handed over for execution [^drhp-p72]. In this model, the contractor does not set prices; it wins work by being the cheapest credible bidder for government and public-sector clients.

That client list is almost entirely government. The major customers named in the filing are the Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai, Western Railway, Central Railway, Mumbai Railway Vikas Corporation Ltd, the Mumbai Metropolitan Region Development Authority, and the Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation of India Ltd, alongside private names such as Sky Way Infrastructure, Dev Engineers, GCC Hotel and Krypton Corp [^drhp-p63]. The company describes itself as a major player in developing infrastructure in Mumbai, slowly consolidating its position in Maharashtra with a strong order book [^drhp-p67].

The client list is also a dependency. Western Railway has been a client since 2006 and contributes 52% of the orders on hand [^drhp-p65-67]. Revenue from key customers amounted to 57% of total revenue in fiscal 2018 [^drhp-p67]. One client relationship, Western Railway, is the backbone of the order book, and the company's own strengths section acknowledges this concentration as a feature of its business model.

<aside class="pull-quote">Western Railway alone accounts for 52% of the orders on hand.</aside>

The company's own list of strengths includes cost efficiency, timely delivery, technical expertise and quality, the use of the box-pushing construction technique, and a leadership team with more than 20 years of infrastructure industry experience [^drhp-p66-67]. Box-pushing is a method for constructing underpasses and tunnels by pushing pre-cast concrete boxes into place beneath live railway tracks, without interrupting train services. The filing also notes heavy investment in machinery and quality-control systems at the RMC facility [^drhp-p67].

## Market and industry context

The DRHP describes the construction sector as highly fragmented and working-capital intensive, contributing roughly 8% to India's GDP at constant prices [^drhp-p58]. Working-capital intensive means the business must tie up large sums in materials, labour and receivables before payments arrive from clients, a structural feature that shapes the company's balance sheet, as the financial section below shows.

The filing's demand story rests on government spending. It cites India's infrastructure investment requirement of ₹50 trillion by 2022, US$24.67 billion of FDI into construction development from April 2000 to December 2017, and a logistics sector expected to grow from US$160 billion in 2017 to US$215 billion by 2020, a CAGR of 10.5% [^drhp-p22-59]. Demand is supported by government flagship programmes such as 100 Smart Cities Mission, AMRUT, Make in India and Power for All; eased FDI norms in 15 sectors including real estate and construction; and rising urbanisation, industrialisation and disposable incomes [^drhp-p58-59].

The headwinds the company lists in its own SWOT are also the sector's: slow pace of government projects, working-capital crunches that may hit profitability, and changes in government policies [^drhp-p76]. Competition is described only as spread across organised and unorganised players; the filing names no specific competitors [^drhp-p76]. The regulatory context the filing sketches includes the Companies Act 2013, GST, the Factories Act 1948, and environmental legislation such as the Water and Air Pollution Acts [^drhp-p78-82].

## The offer and what the money is for

The offer is 44,28,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at an issue price the DRHP has not yet set [^drhp-p29]. The issue section names only the equity shares offered; there is no offer-for-sale line and no selling shareholder in the excerpt, so the issue appears to be entirely fresh shares, meaning all the money raised goes to the company, not to existing shareholders cashing out [^drhp-p29].

The lead manager to the issue is Mark Corporate Advisors Private Limited and the registrar is Bigshare Services Private Limited [^drhp-p31]. The disclosed objects of the issue are: ₹1,130 lakh to meet working capital requirements, and general corporate purposes, for which the amount is not disclosed [^drhp-p48]. The working capital figure is the substantive use of proceeds; the company is raising money to fund its day-to-day operations, not to build a new plant or acquire assets.

The filing also discloses that no independent agency has appraised the proposed project and no monitoring agency has been appointed; fund deployment will be at the discretion of management and the board, monitored by the audit committee [^drhp-p18]. This means the company has not sought an external validation of its plans, and the oversight of how the money is spent rests with internal governance structures.

## Five-year financial history

The restated financials in the DRHP show a shrinking top line and a bottom line that recovered sharply in the latest year. Revenue from operations fell from ₹90.21 crore in FY2015 to ₹60.53 crore in FY2018, a decline of about a third over four years [^drhp-p27]. The full path across the restated period: ₹86.61 crore in FY2014, ₹90.21 crore in FY2015, ₹74.53 crore in FY2016, ₹73.44 crore in FY2017, ₹60.53 crore in FY2018 [^drhp-p27]. The filing reports in ₹ lakh; the figures here are converted to ₹ crore for readability.

Net profit after adjustments was ₹3.15 crore in FY2018, a sharp jump from ₹0.16 crore a year earlier and well above the ₹0.96 crore reported for FY2014. The series: ₹0.96 crore, ₹0.78 crore, ₹0.33 crore, ₹0.16 crore, ₹3.15 crore [^drhp-p27]. The FY2018 profit jump, a twenty-fold increase in one year, is the single most striking number in the financials and comes against a backdrop of falling revenue, which suggests the improvement came from margins and cost control rather than growth [^drhp-p27].

Profit before interest, depreciation and tax (the closest disclosed proxy to EBITDA) moved in line with revenue: ₹7.84 crore in FY2014, ₹9.46 crore in FY2015, ₹7.83 crore in FY2016, ₹6.76 crore in FY2017, ₹9.29 crore in FY2018 [^drhp-p111]. The FY2018 PBDIT of ₹9.29 crore is the second-highest in the five-year period, achieved on the lowest revenue, a sign that the company's cost structure improved in the latest year [^drhp-p27-111].

The balance sheet is short-term-debt heavy. Short-term borrowings rose from ₹30.82 crore in FY2014 to ₹35.37 crore in FY2018, while long-term borrowings fell from ₹5.87 crore to ₹1.33 crore over the same period [^drhp-p26]. Total assets stood at ₹82.32 crore at FY2018 end, up from ₹70.62 crore four years earlier [^drhp-p26]. The pattern is clear: the company has shifted its debt burden from long-term to short-term, which means it is financing its operations with borrowings that must be repaid or rolled over within a year. This is the working-capital-intensive nature of the construction business made concrete: the company needs constant short-term funding to keep projects moving.

The filing itself flags that the company had negative cash flow in recent financial years and warns that sustained negative cash flow could hurt its business and results [^drhp-p16]. The company also had an unsecured loan of ₹405.02 lakh as on March 31, 2018, repayable on demand, meaning a lender could call in the loan at any time, which would hit cash flow [^drhp-p16]. The working capital raise of ₹1,130 lakh is best understood against this backdrop: the company is raising money to reduce its dependence on short-term borrowings and demand-repayable loans.

## Promoter, shareholding and governance flags

The promoter is Amit Bholanath Mishra, who holds 74,95,476 equity shares, 90.949% of pre-issue paid-up equity share capital [^drhp-p100]. The other directors, Bharat Kumar Parmar, Shree Prakash Singh and Mukesh Pandey, each hold just 300 shares [^drhp-p93]. The CFO is Anchal Pachori, a chartered accountant, and the company secretary and compliance officer is Mohit Soni [^drhp-p97]. Hatim Sakerwala holds 9% of the equity shares [^drhp-p98].

Control is concentrated in one person. The promoter holds nearly 91% of the company before the issue, and the three other directors combined hold 900 shares, a rounding error against the promoter's 74.95 lakh [^drhp-p93-100]. After the issue, the promoter's stake will be diluted by the fresh shares, but the pre-issue structure shows a company run by a single controlling shareholder.

Related-party transactions included loans taken of ₹68.4 lakh, director remuneration of ₹57.7 lakh, and payables of ₹324.3 lakh [^drhp-p129-130]. The payables figure of ₹3.24 crore is nearly the same size as the company's FY2018 net profit of ₹3.15 crore [^drhp-p27-130]. The company discloses that it has entered into related-party transactions in the past and may continue to do so in the future [^drhp-p15].

On litigation and conduct, the DRHP says litigation is pending at various stages; it also says no director is on the RBI willful-defaulter list and no criminal cases have been filed against the directors [^drhp-p11-100]. But two companies promoted by the promoter, Al-Noor Chickens Private Limited and A B Magma Automobiles Private Limited, were struck off by the Registrar of Companies in March 2016 for non-operation [^drhp-p18]. The company also discloses that it has issued equity shares in the last 12 months at a price that may be lower than the issue price [^drhp-p16].

## Five material risks in the filing

**Client concentration.** Indian Railways is the largest customer, contributing 57% of revenue in fiscal 2018. Losing it would produce a sharp drop in revenue and profitability [^drhp-p12]. Western Railway alone accounts for 52% of the orders on hand [^drhp-p65].

**Tender dependence.** Revenue depends on winning bids from government and PSU clients, which are awarded to the lowest quote; if bids are not accepted or if order-book contracts are cancelled or suspended, revenue and earnings will be hit [^drhp-p12-72].

**Cash and debt stress.** The company had negative cash flow in recent years, short-term borrowings stood at ₹35.37 crore at FY2018 end, and there was an unsecured loan of ₹405.02 lakh repayable on demand [^drhp-p16-26].

**Geography concentration.** A major portion of revenue comes from operations in Mumbai, and adverse developments in that region could have an outsized impact on revenue and results [^drhp-p13].

**Governance and control concentration.** The promoter holds 90.949% pre-issue, related-party transactions exist, no independent project appraisal or monitoring agency has been appointed, and two promoter-promoted companies were struck off by the ROC [^drhp-p15-100].
